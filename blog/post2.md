# Meditations on being a Lead

I started this post thinking it would be a prescriptive look at what it takes to be a good leader for a team. This excerpt from *Parables of Leadership* summarized perfectly the roadblocks I faced.

> The essence of leadership cannot be reduced to a series of personal attributes nor confined to a set of particular roles and activities. It is like the challenge of describing a bowl: we can describe a bowl in terms of the clay from which it is made. But a true picture must include the hollow that is carved into the clay—the unseen space that defines the bowl’s shape and capacity.

This post is a reflection from my own experience (as an engineering manager, tech lead, and lead for numerous projects) for how I approach leading teams and like to be led. I have been on teams with different leadership styles—and have witnessed how those styles have either uplifted or completely dismantled teams.

## "While washing the dishes one should only be washing the dishes"

This is a quote from Thich Nhat Hanh's book *The Miracle of Mindfulness* that I return to constantly. It's a simple reminder to be fully present in our daily acts so that we can clear our mind and focus on the experience. You'll find even mundane tasks can bring you joy.

This can be incredibly difficult as an engineer—where our stress levels and focus fluctuate from constantly shifting between priorities. I will be the first to admit that I still have days where I struggle as well, but I've worked out ways to cope and manage.

Practicing mindfulness during work has transformed the way I deal with the complexities of leading a team. It's enabled me to relax and clear my mind of clutter and anxieties. By doing so, I am open to think clearly to make decisions that are free of judgment and to give the attention my teammates deserve.

Before meetings, one-on-ones, reviewing technical docs, or PRs, I take a pause to remind myself of this simple mantra.

## “In the beginner’s mind there are many possibilities; in the expert’s mind there are few.”

This quote is a favorite of mine from *Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind* by Shunryu Suzuki. I like to discuss this with teammates because it helps frame the way we evaluate our own success and approach work.

There was a point in my career where work consumed my entire life. I was obsessed with being perfect, having all the right answers, trying to outperform myself and others. I obsessed about code quality and PR reviews, I pulled all-nighters in attempts to master something new, I agonized over whether I said the right things in meetings.

This is what I thought was expected of an engineer, and it paid off. I earned the praise and recognition: 360 reviews were all positive, I got the promotions I deserved. But the reality is I ended up burning out and feeling trapped by my own unsustainable expectations of what it took to be the best engineer, to be an expert.

With a beginner's mind, I learned to slowly unravel and let go of the need to always be right. Instead, I learned to approach situations as an opportunity to learn something new, free from expectations.

I like to share this personal lesson with members of my team:

- To encourage them to let go of their egos and be willing to make mistakes.
- To measure their success by how much they've learned—not in comparison to others.
- To remind them to stay curious.

I think this lesson is especially important for high-performing members of teams who are normally trusted to carry out complex projects.

## “All that you touch You Change. All that you Change Changes you.”

After reading Octavia Butler's *Parable of the Sower*, I was inspired by what positive energy I could bring to the world and how my own actions could affect others for good.

I didn't realize how important leadership was until my own team lead left, who I looked up to, and was replaced by the most senior engineer on the team (as is the case with most organizations). Within months, I went from feeling confident to full-blown imposter syndrome. I was affected by the new leadership’s top-down, militant style of communication and delegation, which ended up creating a less collaborative environment—a culture of blame.

From that point on, I learned what type of leader I wanted to be:

- One who sets the bar high—but meets each individual where they are and sets a sustainable path to where they want to be.
- One who does everything in their power to create the best working conditions and inspires them to be the best versions of themselves.

I realize this sounds like very obvious boilerplate. Let me get specific.

To my two points, *"meeting individuals where they are at"* and *"creating the best working conditions"* means setting a tone of empathy, trust, and encouragement. This gives engineers the mental space they need to focus on creative problem-solving instead of cluttering their minds with external pressure and internal anxiety. *Remember beginner's mind?*

This also means setting clear expectations from the beginning. My usual tenets, which I practice myself, are:

- **Don't make the same mistake twice** (synthesize the information before moving on).
- **Communicate when you are stuck**—your teammates are here to support you.
- **Distribute the knowledge** (documentation or a walkthrough of what you've learned).

I've found—and I'm sure you have too—that I have done my very best work when I'm in environments where I feel trusted. When mistakes are made, they are used as a learning opportunity.

## Golden Ball of Light

Whenever I am incredibly stressed, one way I ground myself is by thinking of the vastness of space. There is a meditation practice I do often where I imagine there's a tiny golden ball of light in the middle of my mind. I slowly watch it expand—seeing the edges of the glowing ball scan the items in the room—to eventually further and further out into the universe.

Reflecting back on teams I have been on—the highest levels of strain have come during times of quick change. When COVID hit, the number of users at Schoology surged to over 1M concurrent users seemingly overnight. Teams were put under enormous strain during our efforts to scale the site.

During this period, we made a safe space for the team to be open about their own personal struggles. I asked openly in teams how this was affecting them, how they managed stress, and made sure to lead by example by being the first to answer.

The aim was to set an open tone for the team—to be open and to be okay with vulnerability. To be honest with themselves when they needed mental health days. This helped the team let go of their own egos:

- To ask for help even when the question seemingly has an obvious answer.
- To be the first to admit when they've made a mistake.
- To be empathetic and uplift when their teammates struggled.

Ultimately, to know that problems they faced were not the sole responsibility of the individual—but of the team as a whole.

## Conclusion

Thanks for joining me in this mind dump. I know my views will further evolve with more experience. I look forward to that change.
